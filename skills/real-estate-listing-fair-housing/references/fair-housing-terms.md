## 1. United States — Fair Housing Act

Seven federally protected classes:

1. Race
2. Colour
3. National origin
4. Religion
5. Sex — including gender identity and sexual orientation under current HUD enforcement
6. Familial status — presence of children under 18, pregnant persons, persons securing custody
7. Disability / handicap

**The operative provision:** 42 U.S.C. § 3604(c) prohibits making, printing, or publishing any statement indicating a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on a protected class.

**Intent is not an element.** Courts apply the *ordinary reader* test — would an ordinary reader perceive a preference? A well-meaning agent writing warm copy can violate this without any discriminatory intent whatsoever. This is precisely why an automated screen has value.

## 2. Common state and MLS additions

These vary widely. Check the local MLS rules; do not assume they apply everywhere.

- Source of income, including Section 8 / housing vouchers
- Age
- Marital status
- Military / veteran status
- Occupation
- Student status (some municipalities)

CRMLS, the largest U.S. MLS, publishes a Fair Housing keyword guide categorising terms into avoid / caution / acceptable. Worth checking directly for the current list in California markets.

## 3. Canada — Ontario Human Rights Code

Fourteen grounds apply to housing under s.2:

Race · ancestry · place of origin · colour · ethnic origin · citizenship · creed (religion) · sex · sexual orientation · gender identity and expression · age · marital status · family status · disability — plus **receipt of public assistance**, which is housing-specific.

**Code s.13** prohibits publishing any notice, sign, or advertisement indicating an intention to discriminate. The OHRC has treated "adult lifestyle" and "adults only" housing advertising as signalling intent to discriminate against families with children.

**The key Ontario divergence:** receipt of public assistance is explicitly protected in housing. Income-screening language — "must earn 3x rent," "no subsidy," "no Section 8 equivalent" — is legally riskier in Ontario than in some U.S. jurisdictions. Flag it firmly for Ontario properties.

---

## 4. Other Canadian provinces

Every province has its own human rights code with differing grounds. **Do not present Ontario's fourteen grounds as universal Canadian law.** If the property is outside Ontario, flag the risky phrase, note that the applicable ground list differs by province, and tell the agent to confirm against their provincial code and real estate board rules.

## 5. Flagged terms and compliant rewrites

| Risk category | Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Familial status** | "perfect for singles," "adult community," "adults only," "no kids," "family-friendly," "bachelor pad," "mature residents," "empty nesters" | State the layout: "spacious two-bedroom," "three bedrooms on one level" |
| **Disability** | "walking distance," "must climb stairs," "able-bodied," "not suitable for wheelchairs," "handicapped," "as-is, needs an active owner" | "close to transit," "ground-floor unit," "elevator access," "three blocks from," "step-free entry" |
| **Race / national origin** | any ethnic descriptor, "exclusive," "traditional neighbourhood," "safe neighbourhood," "up-and-coming," "authentic" area framing | Describe the property. Never characterise the neighbourhood's people or perceived character |
| **Religion** | "near churches / temples / mosques," "Christian community," holiday-specific framing ("perfect for Christmas mornings") | Name the specific amenity factually with a distance, or omit |
| **Age** | "young professional," "perfect for retirees," "senior living" (unless legally qualified), "mature buyer," "starter home for a young couple" | Describe features objectively: "single-level," "low-maintenance lot" |
| **Sex / gender** | gendered "-man" terms, "bachelor pad," "man cave," "she-shed," "master suite" (increasingly avoided by MLSs) | "flex room," "bonus room," "workshop," "primary suite" |
| **Income / public assistance** | "no Section 8," "must earn 3x rent," "no subsidies," "professionals only" | State objective, lawful qualifying criteria consistent with local law — and confirm the jurisdiction first |
| **Sexual orientation / gender identity** | any reference to preferred household composition | Describe the property only |
| **Disability (mental health)** | "quiet building, no drama," "sober living preferred" | Omit; describe soundproofing or building rules factually |

## 6. Proxy language — the subtle category

These phrases name no protected class but function as stand-ins for one. They are the flags agents push back on most, so explain the reasoning rather than just marking them:

- **"Good schools" / "great school district"** — long treated as a race and national-origin proxy in U.S. enforcement. Name the school and the distance, or leave it out.
- **"Safe neighbourhood" / "low crime"** — same proxy problem, plus it is a claim the agent cannot substantiate.
- **"Exclusive," "prestigious," "desirable area"** — signals who is welcome rather than what the property is.
- **"Integrated" or "diverse" neighbourhood** — describing the racial composition of an area is steering even when framed positively.
- **"Walk to church," "close to the synagogue"** — religion proxy unless the amenity is named as a factual landmark with a distance.
- **"Ideal for a growing family"** — familial status, and one of the most common accidental violations in the category.

## 7. Context rules

- The same phrase can be clean in one listing and risky in another. "Walking distance" describing a downtown condo reads differently than "must be able to walk to the parking area." Flag with context, don't blanket-ban.
- Factual, verifiable, property-focused statements are the safe harbour. If a sentence can be checked against the property itself, it is almost always fine. If it can only be checked against the people, it is a flag.
- Accessibility features may be described positively — "step-free entry," "roll-in shower," "36-inch doorways" are helpful facts, not violations. What is prohibited is stating who should or shouldn't apply.
- When the agent's jurisdiction is unknown and the ruling depends on it (income, age, occupation), ask before ruling rather than guessing.
